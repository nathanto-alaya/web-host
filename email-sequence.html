1 · Sat 10 OctAdiNever emailed
SubjectI lost money on my first property
AltThe $213,000 that built this business
PreviewAnd it is the reason Alaya exists at all.

I was 23 when I sat down to sign for a house in Traralgon. $213,000. My hand was actually shaking as I signed, and that number is still seared into my brain.

I sold it 2 years later for $215,000. Once you count what it cost to buy, to hold and to sell, I broke even at best. Realistically I lost money.

The deposit had taken me nearly 5 years to save, working more than 1 job for most of it, and I will be honest with you, the saving part felt humiliating. So to come out the other side with nothing to show was not a great feeling.

Here is what I got wrong, and it was not the timing. I bought in a year that turned out fine for Australian property. What I did not have was a reason. There was no strategy attached to that purchase, I just wanted to be someone who owned a property.

That lesson is the whole reason this business exists, and it is why on 27 October we are running an hour on what the 2027 rules actually reward, rather than on whether now is a good time to buy.

2 · Tue 13 OctRedomAdjacent to 8 Sep
SubjectThe deadline you are being sold expired in May
AltThere is nothing left to beat
PreviewGrandfathering closed on budget night, not in July 2027.

You have probably seen the ads. Buy before July 2027 and keep your negative gearing. Act now, beat the deadline.

That door is already shut. Grandfathering closed at 7:30pm on budget night, 12 May 2026. Any property bought after that moment sits under the new rules from 1 July 2027, regardless of when you sign.

So if you buy tomorrow, you are already inside the new regime. There is nothing left to beat, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling you urgency they do not have.

There is a real date coming, it is just not the one being advertised. On 30 June 2027 every CGT asset is treated as sold and immediately reacquired. No tax is payable on the day, but it splits your gain into an old-rules slice and a new-rules slice, which means having evidence of what your property was worth on that date genuinely matters. That applies to grandfathered owners too.

We are walking through all of it on 27 October, including the part that decides which properties come out ahead.

3 · Fri 16 OctAdiPlaybook only
Subject$73,931 says buy a new build
AltThe carve-out that costs you $109,435
PreviewWe ran it. The tax break loses.

New builds keep negative gearing under the new rules. Established properties do not. So every developer in the country is about to tell you the answer is obvious.

We modelled it properly, because the number does sound convincing at first. Over a 10 year hold, that carve-out is worth $73,931 in tax benefit against your wages.

Then you run the break-even, and this is the part nobody putting it in an ad wants you to see. The new build only has to grow 0.3% a year slower than the established property before the tax benefit is gone.

0.3%. That is the entire margin.

At a 0.5% gap the new build finishes $24,006 behind. At 1.5%, which is what we use to account for builder margin and the new-build premium, it finishes $109,435 behind. That is 30% of the whole result, handed over in exchange for a deduction.

I am not saying never buy a new build. I am saying the tax break is not the reason to, and on 27 October we are putting the model on screen so you can watch the numbers move yourself.

4 · Mon 19 OctAdiNever emailed
SubjectHe bought after 13 straight rate rises
AltEveryone told him he was an idiot
PreviewThe bank valued it 2 years later. Here is the number.

One of my earliest clients bought his first investment property in June 2024, in Bundaberg, for $720,000. He did it after 13 consecutive rate rises, with every single person in his life telling him he was making a mistake.

An independent bank valuation about 2 years later came back at $989,000.

I want to be careful here, because the easy version of this story is that he was brave and everyone else was wrong. That is not the lesson and it is not how we work.

He did not bet on rates. He bought in a market where the population was growing, the job market was holding and there were not enough homes for the people already there. Those things were true whether the cash rate went up again or not, which is exactly why the noise around him did not change the decision.

Rates move the mood. They rarely move the fundamentals underneath a specific market, and on 27 October we are showing you how we tell the difference.

5 · Thu 22 OctAdiNever emailed
Subject34 checks passed. We still walked away.
AltThe 38 metres that killed a good apartment
PreviewWhat our due diligence actually looks like when it fails.

We run 49 checks on every apartment before anyone signs anything. On a property we looked at in Caulfield, 34 came back clear, 12 needed a document or a specialist, and 3 came back below our standard.

Those 3 were not 3 problems. They were the same problem counted 3 ways, and the problem was the road.

The building did everything it could with the position it had. Only 14 apartments, built in 2014, entrance around the corner on the quiet street, and the unit itself on the top floor facing away from the traffic. If you were going to buy on a main road, this was close to the best version of it.

It still did not clear, and the reason has almost nothing to do with noise. You can adapt to noise. What you cannot adapt to is the day you sell, when every buyer who inspects your apartment stands on that same footpath and a meaningful share of them quietly rule it out before they walk in.

Here is the number that proves it. The same layout in that same building took 41 days to sell in 2019. In 2026 it took 203.

You can renovate a kitchen. You cannot move a road. Most of what we actually get paid for is the properties we talk clients out of, and on 27 October we will show you the checklist we use.

7 · Mon 26 OctRedomNever emailed
SubjectTomorrow: the chart that decides what you buy
AltThe reform is not what you think it is
PreviewAt 4% growth you win. At 8% you lose. Here is why.

Most of the coverage of the 2027 changes has treated them as a flat hit to property investors. It is not that, and the difference matters enormously for what you should be buying.

The reform is a tax on nominal capital growth. That single sentence is the whole finding.

Run our base case and it comes out like this. An $800,000 purchase, $600 a week in rent, 80% lending at 6.2%, a 37% marginal rate, held 10 years. Under the old rules the net result is $380,209. Under the new rules it is $364,590. The change costs $15,618, or 4.1%. Considerably less than the headlines suggest.

But hold that same property at 4% to 5% annual growth and the new rules leave you better off than the old ones did. Hold it at 7% to 8% and they cost you real money. Hold it for 30 years and the gap reaches $307,698.

So the rules did not make property worse. They changed which property works, and that is a completely different problem to solve.

Tomorrow, 5pm AEST, Adi and I are putting the model on screen and moving the dial live.
